# Evaluating and citing sources

## Credibility signals
Weigh, don't tally — one strong primary source can outweigh several weak ones.
- **Authority**: who published it, and are they qualified/accountable? Named
  author with credentials > anonymous. Institution/standards body/peer-reviewed
  journal > personal blog > content farm.
- **Primary vs secondary**: prefer the original — the study, the filing, the
  spec, the dataset, the person who was there — over someone summarizing it.
  Follow secondary sources back to their primary.
- **Evidence**: does it show data, methodology, links, and reasoning, or just
  assert? Verifiable beats confident.
- **Transparency**: sources cited, corrections issued, conflicts of interest
  disclosed.

## Recency
- For anything that changes (prices, versions, leadership, law, current events),
  check the publish/update date and prefer current sources. Note the date of the
  info in your answer.
- For stable facts (history, established science), age matters less; authority
  matters more.

## Bias & independence
- Identify the source's incentive: a vendor on its own product, an advocacy org
  on its cause, an outlet with a known slant. Not disqualifying, but discount
  and seek balance.
- **Independence is what makes triangulation work.** Three outlets reprinting
  one press release or wire story = ONE source. Corroborate with sources that
  did their own reporting/analysis. Watch for circular citation (A cites B, B
  cites A) and for AI-generated/SEO content echoing each other.

## Triangulation procedure
1. List each load-bearing claim.
2. For each, find ≥2 genuinely independent sources that support it.
3. If they agree → state as supported (cite both). If they conflict → present
   the disagreement and who says what. If only one → label it single-source.
4. Extraordinary claims need stronger/more sources.

## Red-flag sources
Content farms, undated pages, anonymous claims with no evidence, screenshots
without originals, forums/social posts as sole authority, pages that just echo
the same wording as others. Use them only as leads to a real source.

## Citing
- Cite the specific source you actually opened and read — not a search snippet,
  not "sources say".
- Include enough to locate it: title/publisher and URL (and date for
  time-sensitive facts).
- Attach the citation to the specific claim it supports, inline where practical.
- Quote exactly when wording matters; never paraphrase into a fabricated quote.
- If you cannot find support for a claim, say so — an honest gap beats a made-up
  citation.
